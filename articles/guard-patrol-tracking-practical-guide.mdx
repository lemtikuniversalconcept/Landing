Search for "guard tour system" in Nigeria right now and you'll find active listings for handheld scanning wands, priced from roughly ₦95,000 upward. That's real, current demand — just worth understanding what it does and doesn't solve before you buy into it.

## What the hardware-wand approach actually does

A guard carries a handheld device and taps it against NFC tags or scans QR codes placed at checkpoints along a route. The device timestamps each scan. At the end of a shift, the data gets downloaded — sometimes to a laptop, sometimes to cloud software — and a supervisor can see which checkpoints were hit and when.

This genuinely solves one problem: proving a guard physically reached a location at a given time. For a long time, that was the whole category.

## Where it falls short

**It tells you where, not what.** A scan confirms presence. It says nothing about what the guard observed, whether anything was wrong, or whether they need backup. You still need a separate channel — usually a radio or a phone call — for anything beyond "I was here."

**The data usually isn't real-time.** Many wand-based systems only reveal missed checkpoints after the shift ends, during a review. If a guard skips three checkpoints at 2am, nobody finds out until the morning review — by which point a missed patrol has already cost you whatever it was going to cost you.

**Checkpoints can be gamed.** A tag mounted somewhere accessible without actually walking the route gets scanned without the patrol happening. This isn't a flaw unique to any one vendor — it's inherent to a system where "proof of presence" and "proof the job was done" aren't quite the same thing.

## What a GPS-based, connected approach adds

Phone-based GPS tracking (instead of, or alongside, physical scan points) changes a few things:

- **Missed checkpoints surface immediately**, not at end of shift, so a supervisor can act while it still matters.
- **Location data is continuous**, not just a series of scan points, so you can see the actual route walked, not just the stops along it.
- **It connects to incident reporting directly** — a guard who finds something doesn't need to switch tools; the same device that's tracking the patrol is the one they use to report what they found.

## Which approach is right for you

If your sites are small and your main concern is proving a guard shows up, a scanning wand is a reasonable, low-cost option. If you're managing multiple sites, need to catch a missed patrol while it's still actionable, or want patrol data connected to the same system where incidents get reported, a connected GPS-based platform closes gaps the wand approach structurally can't.
